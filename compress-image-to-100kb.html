Why 100 KB is the number people keep asking for

A 100 KB ceiling shows up in more places than you might expect. Job portals and visa application systems reject resume photos and ID scans that exceed a fixed size, and the limit is often 100 KB or a nearby round number. Government e-filing forms for passport photos, exam registrations, and municipal services enforce similar caps. Webmail providers and workplace mail servers still bounce messages with heavy attachments, so shrinking each image keeps a multi-photo thread deliverable. Content management systems and forums frequently cap single-file uploads too, which bites when you paste images into a CMS instead of linking them from a CDN. A photo straight off a phone camera weighs several megabytes, so it fails every one of those checks until it is compressed.

Why 100 KB specifically? It sits at a useful point on the curve: small enough to clear most form validators, large enough that a standard photograph stays recognizable at normal display sizes. Going lower starts to cost visible quality on photos; going higher defeats the point of a size-limited upload. The target field accepts any whole number of kilobytes up to 10,240, so if a particular form asks for 200 KB or 50 KB, the same passes run against that number instead.

How the compressor reaches the target

No single dial turns an arbitrary image into exactly 100 KB, so the tool works in passes. Starting at the original dimensions, it encodes the image once at a deliberately low JPEG quality. If even that file lands over the target, no quality setting will rescue it at that size, so the tool scales the image down — each pass multiplies both dimensions by 0.8 — and tries again. At the first scale where the low-quality file fits, it runs a short binary search over the quality dial, roughly ten more encodes, and keeps the highest quality that still measures at or under the limit. The result is the largest, sharpest version of the image the byte budget allows, and the size shown in the results panel is measured from the encoded file itself, not estimated from a formula.

Everything happens in your browser: the file is decoded with createImageBitmap, drawn onto a canvas, and re-encoded with canvas.toBlob. No bytes leave the device at any step, which you can confirm in the Network tab. If no scale-and-quality combination fits, the tool stops and says so instead of quietly returning a file that misses the mark. The broader question of when a browser-local pipeline beats an upload service is laid out in the online versus local converter comparison.

The trade-off between dimensions and quality

Two levers control the file size, and they pull against each other. Lowering quality keeps the image at full resolution but adds blocky artifacts around sharp edges, which shows up fastest on text, logos, and line drawings. Scaling the dimensions down keeps each remaining pixel clean but makes the image physically smaller, which matters when a form specifies a minimum resolution or when the picture will be printed. Photographs of people and scenery tolerate quality reductions well; screenshots, documents, and QR codes degrade quickly and are better served by keeping quality high and letting the dimensions shrink. When a form demands a minimum resolution, check the dimensions line in the results panel — that is where a downscale shows up — and rerun with a higher target to keep more pixels.

If the first result looks wrong for your purpose, press Try again and adjust the target rather than accepting the first output. Comparing two runs costs nothing, because there is no server round trip between attempts.

A privacy side effect you probably want

Re-encoding throws away the original file's metadata. EXIF blocks that record the camera model, the capture time, and often the GPS coordinates of where the photo was taken do not survive a JPEG re-encode, because the canvas pipeline writes a clean file rather than copying those tags over. For a personal photo headed to a job application or a government form, that is a genuine benefit: the submitted file carries the pixels, not your home coordinates. Treat this as a side effect rather than a privacy guarantee, though — if scrubbing location data is the actual goal, the dedicated remove photo metadata tool and the remove GPS from photo tool are built for exactly that, and they leave the image pixels untouched.

Compress before you convert, not after

Order matters if the compressed image is headed into a data URI. Base64 encoding inflates binary data by about a third, because every 3 bytes of image data become 4 Base64 characters: a 100 KB JPEG turns into roughly 133 KB of text. If you encode first and compress later, you are working on the wrong artifact — the string, not the image — and the byte budget is gone. The correct sequence is to compress to your target here, download the JPEG, then follow the steps in how to convert an image to Base64 on that file. To know in advance how much text the final file will produce, the Base64 image size calculator gives you the number before you paste anything anywhere.

Limits worth knowing up front

The input accepts JPEG, PNG, and WebP files up to 15 MB and 40 megapixels, and the output is always JPEG. Because the canvas draws onto an opaque white background, transparent areas are flattened to white, and an animated WebP file comes out as a single still frame. Targets use decimal kilobytes (1 KB = 1,000 bytes), the same unit upload forms measure with. And the whole run is local: compression is the kind of job that used to mean finding a website you could trust with a photo, uploading it, and waiting — here the photo stays on your machine from the first encode to the download.
